I don't agree with the comment's closing sentiments. This person can still go and enjoy doing mathematics as a hobby. However, I agree with their sentiment if they're lamenting that they won't be able to enjoy this craft as a job.

I believe in the next 10 years we are going to see a lot of programming jobs disappear. I don't know if they will be replaced partly or fully with other jobs, like the managers or sales roles the comment alludes to. I'm guessing there will be a lot of new work similar to todays Quality Assurance engineers.

I don't think at all that the programming field will disappear.

If that's surprising to you, let me convince you of the essence of a programmer's job.

## It was always about the programming and it was never about the programming

For the past 50 (or more) years we've had a status-quo: to make an executable program you HAD to go employ a programmer.

Then, in a swift time-span of 2 years the AI models have gone from 

1. A semi-helpful chatbot in a separate app, where the programmer could copy and paste text to and from. 
1. A `claude`/`codex` program that can read a project's code or [decode inscrutable error messages](https://gist.github.com/travislbrundage/928a8f7cf6ce49b80e722cbc8899395d). It was then living in the programmer's code editor.
1. A bona fide **agent** that can be given a high-level goal and it will very reliably produce a *working solution*. Nowadays most programmers don't open a code editor app.

The crux of the employability issue is in the *working solution*. 

It completely changes the status-quo: **you do not need a programmer anymore.**

From a manager's perspective the programmer's job in the second phase was clear: the baby sitter. The manager understood that `claude`/`codex` was doing most of the "work". They weren't even mad about it. They were able to close a job posting for the junior role since the baby sitter and `claude`/`codex` took care of the unglamorous work that's usually ignored and instead shoved onto the junior as "learning opportunities": fixing small design discrepancies, chasing down rare issues that were hard to reproduce, updating dependencies, etc.

In the 3rd phase, I'm not even sitting on that baby. It just prints and prints and prints and prints *working* programs faster than I ever could. 

<callout>
On a really productive day I might have written 300 lines of code. <br> An agent can do that in 3 minutes.
</callout>

The thought has probably crossed the manager's mind more than once a day. *The programmer is not needed anymore*. That same cost-trimming-budget-optimization axe that struck the junior's job posting last week was used on the "Senior Software Engineer" job posting today. I'm not great at math but even I can count 1-2-3. 

To be clear, this didn't happen to me but it's a scenario that's playing out throughout the industry. 

From a business perspective, it seems like a no-brainer. It's MUCH cheaper to have the AI produce the code. After all, it. just. works. 

The gotcha here is of course that programmers do more than just write programs. In much the same way that mathematicians do more than "convert coffee to theorems".

## The essence of the programmer, the baker, the plumber, the dry-cleaner

Much better writers have broached the subject before me. See [*"Do You Still Read the Code?"*](https://zanlib.dev/blog/do-you-still-read-the-code/) by Marek Chotoborski and an even earlier work from 1984 by Peter Naur [*Programming as Theory Building*](https://gwern.net/doc/cs/algorithm/1985-naur.pdf).

I will take a more illustrative approach.

I've already mentioned that to make an executable program you needed a programmer before. As such, it's easy to assume that's their _only_ role. This is a fallacy. The executable program is merely an asset that's created. It is not what makes a programmer valuable. Their experience, skills, and context are what make the programmer valueable.

Consider other carreers. 

Is the baker revered because they made good bread? No. The bread is the asset they created. The value of the baker is their bakery and their process. Today's bread is made using the dough mixed yesterday. It's given a day to proof and rise. This is a *logistical nightmare*. How much dough do we need? A bakery sells not one type of bread but up to a dozen, and other baked goods. Each type of bread takes a different amount of time to bake. Some use different doughs. To meet the rush of customers at different times of the day, what is the order of baking and when do they fire? It's a marvel to be able to buy fresh bread everyday.

Is a plumber valueable because they laid pipes in new construction as the build plans dictated? No. A plumber is valueable when they can walk into an 80 year old home, diagnose the cause of a leak, the source of the smell of sewage, the low water pressure, etc. It doesn't matter who built it and whether they used copper pipes, lead pipes, cast iron, plastic, or an unholy amalgamation. The plumber can envision the trade-offs, make all those tiny decisions, and propose a solution in a tidy bill.

Is the dry-cleaner sought after because they just press shirts. Well...probably yes. Most of the time they are cleaning a relatively clean garment. But to truly succeed a dry-cleaner needs to tailor and tackle stains as well. There are different types of fabrics: polyester, cotton, linen, lycra. There are different types of messes like coffee, glue, blood, sweat, jam. Each combination calls for a different chemical treatment. Tailoring is a whole craft, where you have to understand different stiching patterns, materials, and understand how to mend so a new garment fits snug and an old garment fits changing bodies.

Programmers are not unique. They face the same types of problems.

Like the baker, they understand some data has to be pre-processed to be instantly served on demand. A youtube video is uploaded as one file but becomes available to users in many different resolutions, with auto-generated captions in multiple languages. 

Like the plumber, they understand that there's dozens of programming languages, hundreds of frameworks, and thousands of libraries. They see the problem space and craftily choose a set of these to start a project or augment it. 

Like the dry-cleaner, they understand that there isn't just one device for the user but many. Is it a phone, a tablet, a little of both, will it fold, is it a laptop, a desktop, on linux, mac or windows? Each device calls for a different experience to fit.  

## Has your AI ever told you no?

Today, AIs are wonderfully goal-oriented. This science-fiction-level of agency leads people to give them autonomy. This is a mistake.

A key part of autonomy is building your own perspective, your own theory and defending it when it matters. It's very difficult nowadays to provide enough context to an AI system to be able to do that effectively. I believe it will continue to be a significant problem for at least 10 more years. 

We do not like to be told no, but it's necessary. No beautiful snowflake idea ever survives the gritty reality of the real world. Our intentions are seldom aligned with the different grating compounding factors of our messy interconnected world.

> **Customer:** I'm sick of being cold in the winter and hot in the summers. I want to add insulation to my house. Let's add outdoor insulation instead of indoor insulation. This way we avoid encroaching on the limited and valueable internal space
>
> **AI:** Great choice. Tell me the outdoor surface area to cover and what your budget is. I'll search materials with the highest R-value that matches the budget to maximize the insulation!
>
> **Builder:** I highly recommend indoor insulation instead. From my inspection, your house has had humidity problems before. Outdoor insulation tends to trap water vapor, specially the materials with higher R-value. All outdoor materials eventually wear out. This leaves gaps and once water seeps in, outdoor insulation essentially forces the humidity inside. The house cannot breath out the humidity. You will have mold problems and the solution will be expensive repairs on the outside or...switching to indoor insulation.

Note: I'm a programmer, I don't build houses. This is a hypothetical scenario.


The essence of the programmer's work is to take the business's goals and requirements, the intended user experience, the performance budget, the time budget, and the *budget* budget into consideration and make the technological choices. It is to build that context, make the executable programs, and deploy it. Test it live and check assumptions from all these stakeholders. Understand how each part of the program matches to the real world and matches to the design considerations. Then use that understanding to destroy, mend, or create anew when our naive assumptions inevitable crumble under the entropy of the real world.

If you are unconvinced, I *highly* recommend reading [*Programming as Theory Building*](https://gwern.net/doc/cs/algorithm/1985-naur.pdf) by Peter Naur. It's still incredibly relevant despite the fact it was published in 1984.

## So...our jobs are safe?

Are programmers' jobs safe? **Nope**. Well...not the nice cushy roles that we are used to.

The last 20 golden-age years of venture capitalist fueled salaries is probably gone for good.

The vast majority of businesses have no clue what they're doing. They're adulting as hard as we are. Most need to execute faster, iterate, measure, and pivot. A cheap and quickly executing AI is exactly what they need. 

Established businesses will still need programmers. A multi-million dollar company doesn't need to innovate as much as it needs to keep the lights on, be reliable, and maintain their customers trust. They want an expert to make the right choices along those axes. If they instead chose to cost-cut with AIs driven by middle managers, that's their grave to dig.
